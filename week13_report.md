### Question 1: How has your understanding of the exploration–exploitation trade-off evolved with increasing data? How did you balance taking risks by exploring new strategies versus exploiting those that had proven effective?
As our dataset expanded across twelve sequential evaluation cycles to twenty-two observations on our two-dimensional benchmarks and fifty-two in eight dimensions, our comprehension of the exploration–exploitation dilemma evolved from a static heuristic into a dynamic, horizon-aware transition. During early rounds, sparse coverage necessitated risk-tolerant global exploration via quasi-random Latin Hypercube sampling to map unknown basins and prevent premature convergence onto inferior local modes. However, as empirical evidence accumulated, unguided exploratory risks—such as our exploratory probe in Function 4 that dropped to -2.359683 upon stepping outside the positive centroid—demonstrated the severe opportunity cost of late-stage variance seeking. We balanced this tension by coupling an anisotropic Gaussian Process surrogate with a hybrid candidate generator that allocated seventy per cent of candidates to global space-filling designs whilst concentrating thirty per cent within local trust regions around our top three historical optima, enabling sustained exploitation that unlocked consecutive all-time records on Function 6 (-0.089540), Function 7 (3.227650), and Function 8 (9.981970).

### Question 2: As your data set expanded, how did the nature of feedback influence your optimisation process? Can you relate this to how RL agents adjust their reward expectations or update their Q-values?
With the progressive expansion of our observational dataset, the nature of environmental feedback shifted from coarse topological discovery to fine-grained gradient validation, profoundly reshaping how our pipeline prioritised candidate coordinates. Early evaluations frequently produced high-magnitude surprise signals—most notably when Function 5 surged to 8662.482500 at the upper hypercube boundary—whereas later rounds delivered subtler, incremental feedback that refined local lengthscales and compressed posterior variance. When our latest exploratory probe in Function 5 returned 162.900018 in the lower interior corner, the resulting negative reward prediction error immediately depressed the surrogate mean across that quadrant, whereas consistent positive returns in Functions 6, 7, and 8 reinforced high action-value estimates within their respective active subspaces.

### Question 3: Think about the AlphaGo Zero case study. How does your iterative improvement reflect the idea of self-play or autonomous learning? Did your process resemble model-free learning (trial and error) or model-based planning (anticipating future outcomes)?
Reflecting on the AlphaGo Zero paradigm, our iterative optimisation loop embodies autonomous learning by continuously retraining its probabilistic world model on self-generated evaluation trajectories without relying on external domain heuristics. Just as AlphaGo Zero improved by using its neural network to guide Monte Carlo Tree Search and subsequently distilling those search outcomes back into updated network weights, our framework used cross-validated surrogate predictions to select portal queries whose true evaluations subsequently refined kernel hyperparameters and Automatic Relevance Determination lengthscales. Crucially, our methodology operated decisively as model-based planning rather than model-free trial and error, because our strict budget of one query per function per round rendered blind policy-gradient exploration computationally infeasible. By constructing an explicit Gaussian Process posterior over the unit hypercube and evaluating Expected Improvement across thirty thousand simulated candidates prior to each physical query, our pipeline anticipated future informational and reward outcomes before committing to any real-world action.

### Question 4: How could RL strategies be applied to enhance exploration design, efficiency or convergence speed in real-world optimisation tasks?
Reinforcement learning strategies offer transformative potential for enhancing exploration design, sample efficiency, and convergence velocity across complex real-world black-box optimisation tasks such as automated hyperparameter tuning, materials discovery, and industrial process control. First, replacing myopic single-step acquisition rules with multi-step lookahead policies—analogous to deep Q-networks or Monte Carlo rollouts—would allow optimisation engines to value an exploratory query not merely by its immediate expected gain, but by the downstream informational value it unlocks over the remaining query horizon. Second, integrating intrinsic curiosity modules and count-based exploration bonuses alongside Euclidean distance de-duplication can systematically steer agents away from deceptive plateaus, such as the flat zero-response plain observed in Function 1, towards informative boundary transitions. Finally, meta-reinforcement learning and adaptive trust-region scheduling, where an agent dynamically modulates its exploration parameter and perturbation radius based on cumulative reward velocity, can dramatically accelerate convergence onto narrow global optima under tight operational budgets.
